# Curriculum research

Why the app teaches the way it does. This compares maths teaching in England, Japan, Singapore and Finland, plus abacus programmes (UCMAS) and interactive platforms (Brilliant, Beast Academy, Math Academy).

## Key findings

1. **England already borrows from Asia.** Since 2014 the National Curriculum, the NCETM "teaching for mastery" programme, and schemes such as White Rose Maths and Maths No Problem! have been based on Shanghai and Singapore methods. The goal is not to escape the British system. It is to teach the same content more visually, more slowly at the start, and in more depth.
2. **England starts formal maths early.** Japanese children start Grade 1 at 6 and Finnish children at 7. A 6-year-old in Year 1 or 2 in England is asked for work that children of the same age elsewhere still meet through play. Being behind at 6 usually means missing foundations, not missing ability.
3. **Early gaps are small and specific.** Struggling 6-year-olds usually lack one or more of: subitising (seeing small quantities without counting), cardinality (knowing the last number counted is how many there are), part-whole thinking, and bonds to 10. Fixing these unlocks most of what follows.
4. **Japan's make-ten strategy is the most useful single addition.** Japanese Grade 1 builds all addition and subtraction across 10 on splitting numbers around ten (8 + 5 = 8 + 2 + 3). It is visual, it scales up to place value, and English schools teach it only lightly.
5. **Copy how good platforms are built, not just how they look.** Brilliant and Math Academy work because of a prerequisite graph, short interactive problems, mastery gates and spaced review.

## Systems compared

| Approach | How it teaches | Strengths | Limits | Take for the app |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **England** | National Curriculum (2014) by year group; most schools use White Rose small steps; NCETM mastery is widespread | Clear yearly targets, strong arithmetic fluency, public tests | Early formal start; fast pace for children missing foundations; a lot of test preparation | The objective list and test calendar; use the same vocabulary as school (part-whole, bar model, exchange) |
| **Japan** | Sansū Course of Study (2017). Lessons centre on one rich problem: children try it alone, then the class compares methods (*neriage*) and summarises | Deep work on few ideas; make-ten; all times tables by end of Grade 2; strong geometry; soroban in Grades 3-4 | Depends on highly trained teachers and class discussion | Make-ten and break-ten; problem-first lessons ("how many ways?"); early times tables as a stretch goal |
| **Singapore** | Concrete, Pictorial, Abstract (CPA); problem solving at the centre of the framework | The bar model links word problems, fractions, ratio and algebra; PISA 2022 maths 1st (575) | Textbook-heavy; demanding pace in upper primary | CPA in every lesson; the bar model as the main word-problem tool from Stage 1 to KS3 algebra |
| **Finland** | Play-based until 7; trusted, highly trained teachers; little testing or homework | Low anxiety, play and outdoor maths, real-world tasks | PISA maths fell from 548 (2006) to 484 (2022), below the UK (489) | The tone and pacing, not the content |
| **Abacus / UCMAS** | Abacus training, then picturing the abacus to calculate mentally (*anzan*) | Very fast mental calculation; uses visual imagery | Little evidence it transfers to fractions, reasoning or problem solving | The soroban as one place-value scene from Stage 3, as a side module |
| **Brilliant / Beast Academy / Math Academy** | Interactive visual problems; comic-led puzzles; prerequisite graph with spaced repetition | Learning by doing, quick feedback, adapts to the child | Brilliant targets teens and adults; none follows the UK test calendar | The app's architecture |

## Teaching principles from the evidence

- **Concrete, then pictures, then symbols** (EEF; Singapore): each new idea starts with something the child moves, then a picture, then digits, with the supports withdrawn gradually.
- **Reuse a few representations** all the way up. The bar model from age 6 is still used for ratio at 11 and equations at 13.
- **Fluency comes from understanding.** Learn number facts through strategies first (make ten, doubles), then practise them.
- **Mastery before moving on.** Move on when the check is passed on three separate days.
- **Spaced retrieval.** Bring mastered skills back after 1, 3, 7, 21 and 60 days.
- **Short and daily.** 10-15 minutes every day.
- **Talk and explain.** Ask "How do you know?" and "Show me another way".
- **Protect confidence.** No timers at the start; treat mistakes as useful information.

### About "visual learner"

The idea of matching teaching to a preferred learning style isn't supported by research (Pashler et al., 2008). What is well supported is that **every** child learns maths better with good visual and physical models next to the symbols. This app goes further for a child who understands by visualising: **the animation is the explanation.** He should be able to see a concept happen (counters joining, a ten filling up, a rod breaking into ones) before any rule is put into words. See `02-app-plan.md`.

If difficulty with comparing small quantities or subitising persists after months of good practice, ask the school's SENCo about a dyscalculia screen.

## England's test calendar

| When | Assessment | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Reception, first 6 weeks | Reception Baseline Assessment | Statutory, not reported to parents |
| Reception, summer | EYFS Profile (ELGs: Number, Numerical Patterns) | Statutory |
| Year 2, May | KS1 tests | Optional since 2023/24 |
| Year 4, June | Multiplication Tables Check: 25 questions to 12 x 12, 6 seconds each | Statutory |
| Year 6, May | KS2 SATs: arithmetic (30 min) and two reasoning papers (40 min each) | Statutory |
| Year 8 | Diagnostic check | Proposed by the 2025 review |
| Year 11 | GCSE Maths: Foundation (grades 1-5) or Higher (4-9) | Statutory |

The Curriculum and Assessment Review reported in November 2025. A revised National Curriculum is due in 2027 and taught from September 2028. Most primary maths content stays the same.
