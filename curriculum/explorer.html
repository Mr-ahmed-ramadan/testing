The short version

Five findings that shape the app

England already borrowed from Asia.Since 2014 the national curriculum, the NCETM "teaching for mastery" programme and schemes like White Rose and Maths No Problem! have been built on Shanghai and Singapore methods. You don't need to escape the British system so much as teach it more visually, more slowly at the start, and with more depth.
England starts formal maths early.A Japanese child starts Grade 1 at 6, a Finnish child at 7. At 6 your son is in Year 1 or 2 and is being asked for things a Finnish child his age meets through play. Being "behind" at 6 often means missing foundations rather than lacking ability.
The early gaps are usually small and specific.Struggling 6-year-olds usually lack one of a handful of skills: seeing small quantities at a glance, knowing that the last number counted is the total, splitting numbers into parts, and bonds to 10. Fix these and later topics get much easier. The 12 starting objectives below are those skills.
Japan's make-ten method is the best single addition for his age.Japanese Grade 1 builds all adding and subtracting across 10 on splitting numbers around ten (8 + 5 = 8 + 2 + 3). It is visual, it scales up to place value, and English schools teach it only lightly.
Copy how Brilliant and Math Academy are built, not just how they look.What makes these apps work is a prerequisite graph, short interactive problems, mastery gates and spaced review. This inventory is already in that shape: every objective has prerequisites, a visual model and a mastery check.

What the evidence says

Teaching principles for the app

  • Concrete, then pictures, then symbolsEvery new idea starts with something he can move, then a drawing, then digits, with the supports withdrawn gradually (EEF, Singapore).
  • Reuse a few representationsUse one small set of models all the way up. A bar model he learns at 6 still works for ratio at 11 and equations at 13.
  • Fluency comes from understandingNumber facts are learned through strategies (make ten, doubles, near doubles), then practised. Drill comes last.
  • Mastery before moving onHe moves on when the check is passed on three separate days, not when a page is finished.
  • Spaced retrievalMastered skills come back after 1, 3, 7, 21 and 60 days. Most forgetting happens without this.
  • Short and dailyTen to fifteen focused minutes every day work better than an hour at the weekend (EEF recommends daily maths time).
  • Talk and explainAdd "How do you know?" and "Show me another way" prompts. Explaining builds understanding (Japan, NCETM).
  • Protect confidenceNo timers at the start, mistakes treated as useful information, and celebrate effort and strategy. Maths anxiety starts early and lasts.

About "visual learner". Research hasn't supported the idea that teaching to a preferred learning style helps (Pashler et al., 2008). What is well supported is that every child learns maths better with good visual and physical models next to the symbols. So your instinct is right, for a different reason: build the app around pictures and manipulatives because maths is easier to understand that way, and keep the words and symbols alongside them.

If he still struggles with comparing small quantities or subitising after a few months of solid practice, ask his school's SENCo about a dyscalculia screen. Children can't be diagnosed reliably at 6, but early support helps.
